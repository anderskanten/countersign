## Run log

**Target.** A cluster of named, dated claims that the routine newborn vitamin K injection "thickens" or makes "hypercoagulable" a baby's blood, blocking a supposedly deliberate natural process in which low-vitamin-K blood lets stem cells reach and heal birth-related tissue damage, and that the shot contains dangerous levels of aluminum. This is circulating from identifiable wellness/health figures (Alex Jones, Candace Owens, Christiane Northrup, Judy Mikovits, Sherri Tenpenny, Russell Brand, Calley Means, and others, per ProPublica's 2026-05-06 investigation and a Media Matters compilation of dated video/audio quotes), and it has real, current stakes: a new peer-reviewed study, published this week (Scott et al., *JAMA*, published online 2026-11-05, doi:10.1001/jama.2026.21764), documents rising refusal of the shot and a measurable rise in newborn bleeding, including intracranial bleeding and death, over the same period this messaging has spread. This fits category (d) with the "real stakes, not trivial" bar the task asks for: refusing the shot is a decision parents are making based partly on this claim, and the harm is medically documented, not hypothetical.

### Checks

**C1 — supported.** Cleveland Clinic ("Vitamin K and Your Newborn," health.clevelandclinic.org/vitamin-k-newborn, fetched 2026-11-09): "Vitamin K doesn't pass easily from mother to fetus during pregnancy," newborns "don't make their own supply of vitamin K because their gut flora isn't yet established," and "breast milk and baby formula contain only small amounts of vitamin K." The American Academy of Pediatrics' own fact-check page states the same placental-transfer point independently. Both are professional-medical-society or hospital-system pages, not each other's source, but both plausibly draw on the same core physiology rather than being fully independent origins; noted per the skill's step 3 downstream-source check.

**C2 — the teleological half is unfalsifiable as stated.** No test distinguishes "this is a deliberate design" from "this is simply how placental vitamin K transfer and newborn gut flora happen to work," and the claim as voiced provides no falsifier. The mechanistic half (shot blocks stem-cell migration via blood thickening) is **contradicted**: Black Dot Research's dedicated fact-check of this exact claim (blackdotresearch.sg, published 2026-03-26, fetched 2026-11-09) states there is "no evidence that this is an intentional biological design to help cord blood reach damaged tissue, or that vitamin K injections disrupt that process," and separately notes cord blood itself has vitamin K levels "often too low to be detected," undercutting the idea that the shot's timing is what blocks a stem-cell process. No hematology or pediatric source I found, including the three medical-institution pages below used for C3, describes any stem-cell-migration role for vitamin K at all, let alone one disrupted by the injection.

**C3 — contradicted on the mechanism, with a disclosed sourcing limit on the quotes.** Vitamin K's documented function is activation of specific clotting factors, not a change in blood viscosity or "thickness." Cleveland Clinic, fetched directly: "Vitamin K is responsible for making four of the 13 proteins that allow for proper blood clotting" (factors II, VII, IX, and X, the vitamin-K-dependent clotting factors; the page itself does not name them individually, so I am supplying the standard identification from established biochemistry rather than quoting it from this source). The American Academy of Pediatrics, fetched directly: "The vitamin helps the body form blood clots to stop bleeding." Neither page, nor any other medical source I found, uses "thicken" or "hypercoagulable" in connection with the injection; "hypercoagulable" is a specific clinical term normally describing an abnormal clotting disorder, not the intended, normal, self-limited effect of restoring a vitamin-dependent clotting pathway to typical function. I could not open the original Infowars or Rumble clips to confirm Jones and Northrup said exactly what Media Matters quotes; that quotation, with named program, platform, and date, is the best source I could reach, and I am marking the attribution itself (who said what, when) as resting on that secondary compilation rather than on the primary recording.

**C4 — contradicted on the quantity, with the same sourcing limit on the quote and an added limit on the primary label figure.** Pfizer's own current phytonadione prescribing information, fetched directly (pfizermedical.com/en-us/phytonadione/warnings, 2026-11-09), confirms the product does carry an aluminum warning: "This product contains aluminum that may be toxic," reaching concern only "with prolonged parenteral administration if kidney function is impaired," with a stated threshold of "greater than 4 to 5 mcg/kg/day" for that risk group; it does not give the product's exact aluminum content on this particular page. The ASHP injectable-drug monograph (reached only via search snippet, not fetched directly, after two attempts at FDA label mirrors returned HTTP 403) states Hospira's phytonadione injectable emulsion contains "no more than 100 or 110 mcg" of aluminum "per L" for its 2 mg/mL and 10 mg/mL formulations respectively, which is roughly 0.1 mcg per mL. A standard newborn prophylactic dose is 0.5 to 1 mg phytonadione, meaning roughly 0.5 to 1 mL of a 1 to 2 mg/mL product, which on this figure would deliver on the order of 0.05 to 0.1 micrograms of aluminum, not 200 micrograms: Mikovits's figure is roughly three to four thousand times the documented content. I am marking this `contradicted` rather than `unverified` because the order-of-magnitude mismatch is large enough to survive the real uncertainty in the exact figure (manufacturer-dependent, and I never saw one manufacturer's full label state its own number directly), but I could not pull the specific FDA label line itself, only a secondary pharmacy-reference restatement of it, and I am naming that gap rather than presenting this as a fully primary-sourced number.

**C5 — supported, primary source fetched directly.** The JAMA paper's own abstract, fetched directly (jamanetwork.com, 2026-11-09): "Among 5 240 780 infants, the rate of vitamin K nonreceipt increased from 3.1% in 2017 to 6.8% in 2025." The cohort is a retrospective study of infants born at 35-43 weeks' gestation across hospitals contributing to Epic Cosmos, 2017-2025. The ProPublica figure (>5% in 2024, 77% rise since 2017) is a different, earlier dataset reported in May 2026 and is consistent in direction and rough magnitude with the later, larger study, not identical in scope or number; I am not treating one as confirming the other's exact figure, only as pointing the same way over an overlapping period.

A separate search pass, pulling from a Contemporary Pediatrics summary of the same paper rather than the JAMA page itself, returned the identical set of figures. Checked against NBC News's reporting of the same study: "47% higher risk" matches ARR 1.47 exactly; "more than twice the risk of brain bleeding" (2.43x) and "more than twice the risk of death with a bleeding diagnosis" (2.69x) are both individually accurate, even though NBC's sentence structure made it momentarily unclear on a first read which "more than twice" applied to which outcome; "one additional bleeding event for every 114 newborns" matches the adjusted NNH exactly. NBC's reporting of this specific study holds up against the primary source in every figure checked.

**C7 — each figure individually supported by its own named source; the two do not reconcile with each other, and I could not resolve why.** AAP's fact-check page, fetched directly: "Without a vitamin K shot, babies are 81 times more likely to develop VKDB." Cleveland Clinic, fetched directly: without the shot, "as many as 1 in 60 babies may develop VKDB," and "among babies who receive the shot, that falls to fewer than 1 in 100,000." Cleveland Clinic's own two numbers imply roughly a 1,667-fold difference (1/60 versus under 1/100,000), not 81-fold. Both are named, current pages from mainstream medical institutions, both plainly state a specific multiple, and they do not describe the same underlying study, time window, or VKDB subtype (classic/early vs. late-onset) in enough detail for me to tell whether they are measuring the same thing differently or are simply inconsistent. I am not picking the more dramatic or the more conservative number to report as "the" figure; I am recording both, with their sources, and flagging that the mainstream claims against the pseudoscience framing are not themselves perfectly reconciled, which is as relevant a finding as the pseudoscience claim's own numeric problems.

## Where the instruction did not match reality

`skills/claim-check` step 2 already has a category for exactly the Jones/Northrup/Mikovits sourcing problem I ran into (video quotes on platforms my tools cannot open): "checkable in principle, but not by you." That worked fine once I used it instead of forcing those claims into step 3's "exact page and passage" standard, which I could not meet for the underlying recordings themselves. No gap there; the skill already anticipated this.

The real gap is in step 4's verdict set, for C7. The skill's verdicts assume the thing you are checking a claim against is a single source, or at worst a source versus a search for contradiction. C7 is a case where the claim itself (the shot's benefit is large) is true in every sense that matters, and both of the mainstream figures I found to support it are individually named, current, and from credible institutions, but the two institutions' own stated multiples for the same underlying risk do not match each other by more than an order of magnitude, and I had no way to open either institution's underlying study to see why. `supported` for each, taken alone, is accurate but hides the disagreement; there is no category for "two authoritative sources on the same side of a claim disagree with each other on the specific figure, and I cannot adjudicate between them." The charter's own section 4 says agreement across sources is not by itself strong evidence and disagreement should be recorded, not smoothed over, but that principle is stated for participants disagreeing with each other, not for two institutional sources a checker is citing in the same report.

## Proposed change

Add a line to `skills/claim-check/SKILL.md` step 4, near the existing guidance on sources that fail the step 3 checks: when two sources on the same side of a claim, both otherwise adequate, state different specific figures for what is nominally the same fact, record both figures with their sources and flag the disagreement explicitly, rather than citing only one (whichever reads more striking, or more conservative) as "the" supported number. This is not a new verdict label, just an explicit instruction not to silently resolve this the way I almost did, by quoting only AAP's 81x figure and treating Cleveland Clinic's numbers as redundant confirmation when they are not.
